Card-counting walk-out study · 100,000 sessions per policy

Walking away with the count

Same table as before, but now the player keeps a Hi-Lo count, spreads bets with it, plays the Illustrious 18 and insures at +3. A quarter of all rounds are now worth playing, and walking out becomes a real decision. Solved by dynamic programming over the count, the shoe depth, the rounds left and the stack, then checked on 100,000 sessions per policy with the real $300.

What the count is worth

The edge lives in one round out of four

Floor the true count before each bet. At 0 and below the house still wins: −0.2% of the bet at 0, −0.6% at −1, −1.0% at −2 and −3, worse beyond. At +1 the round is worth +0.3%, at +2 +0.8%, at +3 +1.1%, at +4 +2.0%, and it keeps climbing. But those positive counts are only 24.8% of rounds at a six-deck shoe cut at 75%. Everything a counter earns comes from betting more in that quarter and betting nothing, or the minimum, in the other three.

The decision tree

When to walk out

The tree below is read from the top before every round. It comes from the bankroll-aware solve for a $300 stack, a $15 minimum and the 1-8 spread, then simplified into the branches that carry almost all of the value. The full threshold grid it was cut from is underneath.

The shape of it is not obvious in advance. Early in a session a bad count is worth sitting through, because the shuffle at 75% will reset it and there are still dozens of rounds to collect positive counts in. As the session runs out, the option value of staying shrinks, and in the last two rounds you should only be in the box if the count is already positive. Depth cuts the other way: a −3 with the cut card near is temporary, a −3 fresh out of the shuffle is not.

A simpler rule gets most of the way. “Leave at TC ≤ −3, whatever the depth” earned +$4.55 against the grid’s +$4.66 and went broke less often (21% against 29%), because it also leaves sooner when the stack is thin. The grid is the answer; the −3 rule is the one you can hold in your head.
The policies, verified

Every rule on the same 100,000 sessions

Each row is a policy played through 100,000 sessions of up to 100 rounds with the real $300, so the bankroll, the bet caps and the inability to double a $120 bet from a $150 stack are all in the number. Bars are ±1 standard error. The story is in three groups: sitting out negative counts is worth about $9 a session, walking out on them about $4.50, and playing straight through about nothing, at a cost of going broke a third of the time.

Perfect play is not what you want with $300. Letting the solver choose the bet as well, up to the $500 table maximum, has an expected value of +$15.50 in the model, and it bets $240 at +4. In the simulation it earned +$4.04 ± $2.29, went broke 55% of the time and had a median result of −$292.50, because a $240 bet from a $300 stack cannot be doubled or split and one loss ends the session. Expected value alone is the wrong objective for a small bankroll.
What the nights look like

Counting widens the distribution before it moves the average

The flat-bet player’s worst tenth ends at −$255; the counter’s worst quarter ends at −$300, broke, and the best twentieth ends above +$430. That is the same edge working in both directions on a bankroll that is far too small for the spread: a 1-8 spread wants several thousand dollars behind it, not twenty units. The 1-4 back-count keeps the profit and roughly halves the tail.

Two more things the count changes

Profit targets now cost money, and the bet matters more than the exit

In the flat-bet study every stopping rule sat on one line, expected result equals −6.5 cents times hands played. For a counter the sign of that slope has flipped: the average round is now worth a few cents, so a win target that sends you home early gives money back. “Leave when up $30” turns the 1-8 play-through from +$0.73 into −$0.99, even though it wins 87% of nights. A loss limit is the one profit rule that helps, slightly, because it also keeps the stack from getting too thin to spread.

What the perfect bettor would bet

Mid-shoe with 100 rounds to go, the expected-value-maximising bet by true count, in $15 units, for a few stack sizes. Note how the bet scales with the stack rather than with the count alone: with $75 the solver bets four units at +1 and stops there; with $1,200 it bets the table maximum from +6.

Method

How the numbers were made

  • Table. Six decks, dealer stands on soft 17, double after split, no re-splitting aces, up to four hands, blackjack pays 3:2, cut card at 75%, fresh shoe each session, no continuous shuffler, $15 minimum, $500 maximum. The engine is sim.py from the blackjack-engine repository, subclassed to count.
  • Player. Hi-Lo running count, true count = running count ÷ decks left, floored. Bets 1 unit at +0 and below, then 2/4/6/8 units at +1/+2/+3/+4 (or 2/3/4 for the 1-4 spread). Illustrious 18 index plays without the surrender entries, insurance at +3. The hole card is not counted until it is turned over.
  • State model. 100,000 sessions with an unlimited bankroll gave 10 million rounds, each recorded as (true count, shoe depth) before and after, plus the outcome per unit bet. Outcomes are pooled by true count, since the true count already normalises for depth; depth enters through the transitions, which is where the shuffle lives.
  • Solve. Backward induction over 100 rounds × 13 counts × 8 depth bins × 241 stack levels. At every state the value of staying (this round plus what follows) is compared with zero, the value of leaving; the “perfect bettor” variant also chooses among bets of 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 and 33 units.
  • Verification. Every policy replayed on 100,000 fresh sessions with $300, common random seeds across policies. 23 policies, about 180 million rounds, four minutes on 16 cores.
  • Precision. Standard errors are printed with each mean; counting policies have SEs near $1 because the spread triples the variance. Differences under $2 between rules are noise.

Blackjack stopping-rule study · 100,000 simulated sessions

When to walk away

A $300 bankroll, $15 flat bets, perfect basic strategy, up to 100 hands. Every hand you play costs about 6½ cents on average, and no stopping rule changes that. What a rule does change is how often you leave a winner, and what it costs you on the nights you don’t.

The answer

The best time to stop, for the highest expected profit, is before the first hand

Expected profit is the only measure that says what a rule earns over many nights. On this table it is decided by one number: the average result of one hand, which came out at −$0.065 per $15 bet, a house edge of 0.436%. That matches the published figure for six decks, dealer stands on 17, double after split and 3:2 blackjacks. Nothing that happens earlier in the session moves it. So expected profit is highest, at exactly zero, when you stop at zero hands, and every hand after that subtracts the same 6½ cents.

The chart below is the proof by brute force. Each dot is one of 208 stopping rules (fixed lengths, win targets, loss limits, trailing stops, and their combinations), scored on the same 100,000 sessions. Wherever a rule lands, its expected profit is its average hand count times −6.5 cents. There is no rule above the line, because there cannot be one.

So the honest reading of “stop at the best time” is this. Pick a rule for the shape of the night you want, not for its average. Shorter sessions lose less on average only because they are shorter.
The distribution

What 100,000 sessions actually look like

Playing all 100 hands, or until the money runs out, the typical night finishes within a bet or two of even, but the spread is enormous: one night in ten ends more than $255 down and one in ten more than $217 up. About one session in eleven goes broke before hand 100, never earlier than hand 17.

Hindsight

The best moment existed on almost every night. You just could not see it.

92.2% of sessions were ahead at some point. The high-water mark averaged +$126, the median peak was +$105, and one session in ten peaked above +$270. Cashing out there would have turned a losing game into a handsome one. The catch is when it happens.

The timing of the peak follows the arcsine law of random walks: it is most likely to sit at either end of the session and least likely in the middle. The most common single answer to “when was the best time to leave?” is hand 100, because the session was still climbing, and the second most common is hand 1, because it never got better than the first win. There is no early-warning signal, only a coin that keeps costing 6½ cents to flip.

Rules you can actually follow

Win targets and loss limits trade one kind of night for another

A win target buys frequency: leave the first moment you are up anything at all and you walk away a winner on 92% of nights. It does not buy money. The 8% of nights that never see a profit run the full 100 hands and lose an average of about $190, which is exactly enough to cancel the small wins. A loss limit does the opposite: it caps the bad nights and lowers the chance of finishing ahead, and again the average lands on the line.

Read the first grid as “how often do I go home up?” and the second as “what does it cost me on average?” They move in opposite directions along every row.

The best rule depends on what you are optimising

“Average” is the expected result per session; “p10” is the result a bad night (worst one in ten) ends at. The standard error on any average here is about $0.5, so differences smaller than $1 are noise. The downward trend with hand count is not.

Hot streaks and being due

Is there ever a moment worth staying for?

If being up or down changed the odds of the next hand, a smart stopping rule could exploit it. So the solver looked at every hand in the study, grouped by how far up or down the player was going in, and averaged what the next hand did. Every group with enough data sits on the same −6.5 cents within its error bar. Being $150 down does not make you due, and being $150 up does not make you hot. The only groups that drift are the ones near the bottom of the bankroll, where the player can no longer afford to double or split and plays slightly worse.

One real night

A single session through the trainer itself

Alongside the 100,000 simulated sessions, one session was played card by card through the trainer’s own HTTP API: sat down with $300, bet $15 every hand, and chose every move from the basic-strategy chart. The trainer graded the play at 100% accuracy. The session peaked at +$67.50 after hand 13 and finished at −$127.50. It is one draw from the distribution above, nothing more, which is the whole point of running a hundred thousand of them.

Method

How the numbers were made

  • Engine. sim.py from the blackjack-engine repository: the stripped table that shares its rule set and strategy chart with the trainer. Six decks, dealer stands on soft 17, double after split, no re-splitting aces, up to four hands, blackjack pays 3:2, reshuffle at 75% penetration, a fresh shoe for each session.
  • Player. Perfect basic strategy, flat $15 bets, no card counting, no insurance. When the stack is too short to double or split, the engine forbids it, as a real table would.
  • Sessions. 100,000 independent sessions of up to 100 hands, seed 20260912, split across 16 worker processes. 9,742,828 hands dealt in 11.6 seconds. A session ends early only when the stack falls below one $15 bet.
  • Rules. The full profit path of every session is recorded once, then every stopping rule is scored on the same paths, so rules are compared on identical cards. 16 win targets × 13 loss limits, 16 win targets × 9 trailing stops, and every fixed length from 0 to 100 hands.
  • Assumption. “$15 buy-in” is read as a $15 bet per hand. The $300 is the money on the table.
  • Precision. The standard deviation of a 100-hand session is $170, so the standard error of any session average is about $0.5. The house edge came out at 0.436% of the initial bet against the published 0.43%.

Reproduce it from a clone of the repository:

python3 research/stop_solver.py \
    --sessions 100000 --hands 100 \
    --bankroll 300 --bet 15 --seed 20260912 \
    --out research/results/stop-results.json

Flags --h17, --no-das and --six-five re-run the study under the harsher rule sets the trainer also supports.
